How to Know if You're Being Underpaid in 2026

LifePera·August 5, 2026

A salary title alone cannot tell you whether your pay is competitive. A useful comparison needs to match the work, location, experience, pay period, and total compensation being compared. This guide gives you a repeatable way to gather that information; it does not estimate a universal market rate or determine what an employer should pay you.

Define the role before comparing pay

Write down your main responsibilities, decision-making scope, specialist skills, years of relevant experience, industry, and work location. Job titles are inconsistent between employers, so compare the duties and level of responsibility as well as the title. If you work remotely, note whether the employer's pay range depends on your location.

Build a like-for-like comparison

Use more than one recent source where possible, such as job postings that disclose pay, a public labor-statistics source, a professional association report, or a transparent employer pay band. Record the source, publication date, geography, occupation definition, and whether it reports a median, range, or average. Treat self-reported salary websites as one input, not a verified measure.

For U.S. occupations, the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics. Those estimates describe occupations across defined areas and do not establish the right salary for a particular person, employer, or offer. Outside the United States, look for the equivalent official statistics agency and check how it defines pay and occupation.

Compare the whole compensation package

Separate guaranteed cash from variable or conditional compensation. List base pay, expected or target bonus and its conditions, equity and vesting schedule, retirement contributions, insurance, paid leave, and any other benefits that matter to you. Do not treat a target bonus or unvested equity as cash already received. Convert pay to the same time period and currency before comparing, and note any assumptions.

Look for evidence, not a single warning sign

A colleague's salary, a recruiter message, an expanded workload, or a raise below inflation can prompt questions, but none alone proves that your pay is below a relevant market rate. Check whether the comparison uses the same location, level, responsibilities, and compensation components. If sources disagree, preserve the range and explain the difference rather than selecting the highest figure.

Prepare a grounded conversation

Keep a short record of responsibilities added, results you can substantiate, and the comparison sources you used. Ask your manager how pay is set for your role and what criteria, process, or review timeline applies. A practical opening is: ?Could we review how my current scope and compensation align with the range for this role? I have gathered comparable information and would like to understand the criteria and next steps.?

If you are given a range, ask what would move compensation within it and when progress can be reviewed. Do not claim a quantified result unless you can document it. Company policies, local law, bargaining agreements, and individual circumstances differ; seek qualified local advice if you need legal guidance.

Make the decision with your own constraints in view

A compensation gap is only one part of a job decision. Consider stability, benefits, schedule, location, caregiving needs, health, and the risks and costs of changing roles.
