Can You Drink the Tap Water? A Guide for 50 Countries

You have unpacked your bags after a 12-hour flight, collapsed into your hotel room, and realized you are parched. You walk over to the bathroom sink, turn the faucet, and pause. Can you actually drink this tap water?

It is one of the most common questions travelers face, yet the answer is rarely a simple yes or no. Drinking tap water in the wrong country can instantly ruin a bucket-list trip with severe gastroenteritis, parasitic infections, or bacterial stomach bugs. Conversely, buying single-use plastic bottles in countries where tap water is perfectly pure wastes money and damages the environment.

To help you navigate your next journey safely, we built this comprehensive travel guide covering tap water safety across 50 popular travel destinations worldwide, along with practical tips on water purification and health precautions.

What Determines Tap Water Safety?

When we ask if tap water is safe, we are looking at two distinct factors: microbiological safety and mineral composition.

In high-income nations with advanced infrastructure, municipal treatment facilities filter out harmful pathogens like E. coli, Giardia, Cryptosporidium, and salmonella. They also maintain strict distribution networks that prevent pipe contamination before the water reaches your glass.

In many developing regions, water treatment may be insufficient, or aging underground pipes can leak heavy metals, industrial pollutants, and bacteria back into purified water. Furthermore, local residents in some countries may drink the water with zero issues because their digestive systems have adapted to local micro-flora over time. As a traveler, your gut lacks those specific immunities, making you far more vulnerable to foreign bacteria.

The 50-Country Tap Water Safety Matrix

We have categorized 50 of the world’s most visited destinations into three safety profiles: Safe to Drink, Exercise Caution / Regional Differences, and Avoid Drinking (Stick to Purified).

1. Safe to Drink Directly from the Tap

In these countries, tap water meets or exceeds strict international public health standards set by the World Health Organization (WHO). You can freely drink, brush your teeth, and cook with tap water here without secondary treatment.

  • Iceland: Sourced directly from glaciers and springs, Iceland boasts some of the cleanest tap water in the world.
  • Switzerland: Renowned for pure alpine tap water that tastes better than most bottled brands.
  • Norway: Fresh, delicious, and rigorously tested tap water across the entire nation.
  • Finland: Tap water here is often cleaner than bottled options due to strict natural filtration protection.
  • Sweden: Tap water is completely safe, clean, and delicious everywhere.
  • Denmark: Sourced almost entirely from deep groundwater, requiring minimal chemical treatment.
  • Netherlands: Uses advanced non-chlorine purification technology to deliver exceptional tap water quality.
  • Austria: Tap water comes directly from alpine springs in major cities like Vienna.
  • Germany: Regulated by strict federal laws; tap water is clean and safe to drink everywhere.
  • United Kingdom: Highly safe municipal water systems across England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland.
  • Ireland: Safe across cities and rural networks, though minor hard water minerals are common.
  • France: Fully safe and drinkable, though water hardness varies by region.
  • Canada: Pristine tap water across urban areas and vast majority of rural towns.
  • United States: Safe in major cities and towns, though taste varies based on local mineral content.
  • Japan: Features an extraordinarily advanced filtration network; tap water is completely safe.
  • Singapore: World-class urban water recycling and purification make tap water safe and clean.
  • South Korea: Highly purified tap water nationwide, though many locals still prefer home filters for taste.
  • Australia: Safe, strictly tested tap water available in all cities and major regional hubs.
  • New Zealand: Exceptionally clean water in cities and towns, derived from mountain runoff and rain.
  • Belgium: Fully safe and drinkable, following strict European Union sanitary regulations.

2. Exercise Caution or Check Local Infrastructure

In these destinations, tap water quality varies by municipality, seasonality, or age of building plumbing. While major cities or tourism zones may treat water properly, rural areas or older buildings with lead/copper pipes can pose health risks.

  • Spain: Safe to drink in major cities like Madrid and Barcelona, but coastlines and islands can taste highly chlorinated or brackish.
  • Italy: Generally safe in urban areas, but older plumbing in historic city centers can leach minerals. Look for public drinking fountains marked "Acqua Potabile".
  • Greece: Tap water is safe in Athens and major mainland hubs, but avoid tap water on smaller islands where desalinated water is piped through fragile networks.
  • Portugal: Tap water is compliant with European safety standards, though coastal regions often have a heavy chlorine taste.
  • Croatia: Tap water is safe in major towns along the coast and in Zagreb, though rural island wells require caution.
  • Poland: Modernized municipal water is safe, though older apartment complexes may affect flavor and mineral content.
  • Costa Rica: Tap water is safe in major urban areas like San José and developed tourist zones, but avoid drinking tap water in remote coastal regions.
  • Chile: One of South America's safest tap water networks, though high mineral content in Santiago can cause upset stomachs for sensitive travelers.
  • United Arab Emirates: Tap water is desalinated and safe to drink, but high temperatures and private holding tanks lead most residents to prefer bottled or filtered water.
  • Qatar: Desalinated tap water is microbiologically safe, but long storage in private roof tanks means bottled or filtered water is widely preferred.

3. Avoid Drinking Tap Water (Stick to Filtered or Bottled Water)

In these countries, tap water frequently contains pathogens, parasites, heavy metals, or volatile chemical runoff that can cause serious illness in travelers. Use boiled, bottled, or UV-purified water for drinking and brushing teeth.

  • Mexico: Tap water is unsafe due to distribution system contamination; stick to bottled or purified ice.
  • India: High risk of waterborne illnesses including typhoid and cholera; avoid unboiled tap water entirely.
  • Thailand: Water leaving treatment plants in Bangkok is treated, but aging pipes cause contamination before reaching taps.
  • Vietnam: Tap water contains high bacterial loads and potential heavy metal contamination.
  • Indonesia: Avoid tap water in Bali and Jakarta; always opt for sealed bottled or micro-filtered water.
  • Egypt: Untreated tap water carries parasites and high chlorine levels; rely on bottled options.
  • Morocco: City water is heavily chlorinated, but contamination in rural areas makes bottled water the safest choice.
  • South Africa: Safe in major cities like Cape Town and Johannesburg, but unsafe across rural areas, so caution is required countrywide.
  • Peru: High incidence of parasites and bacterial pathogens in tap water throughout the country.
  • Brazil: Filtered water is standard in local homes; unboiled tap water should be avoided by visitors.
  • Colombia: Safe in central Medellín and Bogota, but unsafe throughout the rest of the country; avoid tap water elsewhere.
  • Philippines: Tap water is unsafe for foreigners; stick to purified water dispensers and sealed bottles.
  • Kenya: Municipal water treatment is unreliable; drink only tested, purified, or bottled water.
  • Turkey: High chlorine content and old delivery infrastructure mean bottled water is recommended everywhere.
  • Dominican Republic: Unsafe tap water; resort areas usually provide purified water systems for guests.
  • Jamaica: Tap water in main resorts is technically treated, but pipeline issues make bottled water far safer.
  • Guatemala: Parasitic contamination is widespread in untreated surface and tap water.
  • Nepal: High bacterial contamination risks; rely entirely on boiled or UV-filtered water.
  • Cambodia: Avoid tap water nationwide due to heavy metals and microbial hazards.
  • China: Boiling tap water is standard practice across the country, as raw tap water is not safe to drink anywhere.

Hidden Tap Water Risks Travelers Overlook

Drinking a glass of water isn't the only way tap water pathogens enter your system. When traveling through high-risk countries, keep these subtle water hazards in mind:

  • Ice Cubes: In many street food stalls and lower-budget eateries, ice is frozen from raw tap water. Stick to high-end establishments or ask for drinks without ice. Look for ice with hollow centers, which typically indicates commercial factory production.
  • Brushing Teeth: Rinsing your toothbrush under a tap in high-risk zones is an easy way to swallow active bacteria. Keep a bottle of purified water on your bathroom counter specifically for mouth rinsing.
  • Fresh Produce: Raw salads, unpeeled fruits, and herbs washed in contaminated tap water can carry parasites. Stick to cooked vegetables or fruits you can peel yourself (like bananas or oranges).
  • Showering: Keep your mouth firmly closed while showering, and be especially cautious when bathing young children.

How to Purify Water on the Go

If you want to reduce single-use plastic waste while traveling through countries with unsafe tap water, carrying your own purification setup is a smart, eco-friendly solution.

  • UV Light Purifiers: Devices like SteriPEN use ultraviolet light to neutralize viruses, bacteria, and protozoa within 90 seconds. They require clear water to be effective.
  • Hollow-Fiber Membrane Filters: Straw filters and integrated filter bottles (such as Sawyer or LifeStraw) physically strain out bacteria and parasites as you drink.
  • Boiling: Bringing water to a rolling boil for at least one full minute (three minutes at high altitudes) kills virtually all biological pathogens.
  • Purification Tablets: Chlorine dioxide or iodine tablets are lightweight and cost-effective for emergency backup, though they leave a distinct chemical taste and take 30 to 240 minutes to work fully.

What to Do If You Fall Ill

Despite your best precautions, accidental exposure happens. If you develop traveler's diarrhea, nausea, or abdominal cramping, act quickly:

  • Stay Hydrated: Sip oral rehydration salts (ORS) mixed with guaranteed clean, bottled water to restore lost electrolytes.
  • Avoid Anti-Motility Drugs Initially: Medications like loperamide can trap dangerous bacteria in your digestive tract longer. Use them only if you must board a long transit flight.
  • Seek Medical Care: If you experience high fever, bloody stools, or severe dehydration lasting longer than 48 hours, visit a local medical clinic for a proper prescription of antibiotics or antiparasitics.
