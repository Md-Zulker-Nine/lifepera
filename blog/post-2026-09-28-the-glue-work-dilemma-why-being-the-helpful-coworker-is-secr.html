The Glue Work Dilemma: Why Being the 'Helpful Coworker' Is Secretly Stunting Your Career (And How to Get Recognized)

Zulker Nine·September 28, 2026

Imagine this scenario: It is performance review season. Over the past year, you have been the undisputed backbone of your team. When onboarding docs were outdated and confusing, you stayed late to rewrite them. When cross-departmental communications broke down, you stepped in to mediate. You organize the sprint retrospectives, take meticulous notes in every meeting, help junior colleagues debug their projects, and fix messy operational processes before anyone else even notices they are broken. Everyone loves working with you. Your manager constantly calls you "the glue that holds this team together."

Yet, when promotion announcements go out, your name is nowhere on the list. Instead, the promotion goes to a colleague who rarely speaks in team chats, never volunteers for administrative duties, and consistently declines requests to mentor new hires. Why? Because that colleague spent 100% of their bandwidth shipping high-visibility, metric-driven projects, while you spent half of yours keeping the ship afloat.

Welcome to the Glue Work Dilemma. Coined by technical leader Tanya Reilly, "glue work" refers to the crucial, highly necessary tasks that keep a team running smoothly, but rarely count toward career advancement. While glue work is essential for organizational success, relying on it to prove your worth is one of the stealthiest ways to stunt your career growth, freeze your salary, and burn yourself out.

In this guide, we will unpack why being the helpful coworker is secretly holding you back, how to spot when you are trapped in invisible labor, and the exact strategies you need to transition from unrewarded "team glue" to recognized, high-impact leader.

What Is Glue Work and Why Is It So Dangerous?

Glue work encompasses all the non-promotable tasks (NPTs) required to maintain a healthy, functioning workplace. It is the connective tissue of an organization. Without it, projects stall, communication fractures, and workplace culture deteriorates. However, there is a fundamental mismatch between what organizations need to function and what organizations reward during career reviews.

Common examples of glue work include:

  • Meeting hygiene: Taking detailed notes, tracking action items, setting up agendas, and facilitating discussions.
  • Onboarding and mentorship: Guiding new hires, answering endless process questions, and writing internal Wiki documentation.
  • Process smoothing: Reconciling conflicting priorities between departments, managing team morale, and fixing broken workflows.
  • Administrative housekeeping: Organizing team events, managing Jira boards, scheduling cross-functional syncs, and chasing people down for updates.

The danger of glue work is not that the work itself is bad—it is that it operates as an invisible tax on your time. If you spend 15 hours a week doing glue work, you only have 25 hours left to work on the core, quantifiable deliverables that determine your performance rating. Meanwhile, your peers who ignore glue work spend all 40 hours on tasks that directly generate revenue, launch products, or reduce costs.

When evaluation time comes, leadership looks at metrics, code written, deals closed, or feature launches. They rarely measure "times you prevented a team crisis." As a result, the glue worker is praised verbally for being a great "culture fit," but passed over financially for those who produced tangible, headline-worthy results.

The Hidden Traps of Being the Unofficial Team Caretaker

The Glue Work Dilemma goes deeper than just missed promotions. Over time, taking on too much invisible labor creates severe structural hazards for your career trajectory and mental well-being.

1. The Gender and Personality Imbalance
Research published in the Harvard Business Review reveals that women and individuals high in agreeableness are significantly more likely to be asked to take on non-promotable tasks, and are also far more likely to agree to do them. When volunteering for low-impact work becomes expected of specific individuals, it compounds systemic pay and promotion gaps over time.

2. The Indispensability Paradox
When you become the sole person who knows how to navigate internal bureaucracy, onboard candidates, or resolve team friction, you make yourself too indispensably operational to be promoted. Management fears promoting or transferring you because doing so would leave an operational void they cannot easily fill. You become trapped in your current role precisely because you do it too well.

3. The Expertise Gap
While you are busy coordinating schedules and drafting documentation, your technical or core professional skills may start to atrophy. While your peers are mastering new software tools, acquiring certifications, or building complex strategic portfolios, your portfolio remains filled with operational maintenance. Over time, this erodes your competitive edge in the job market.

4. Resentment and Quiet Burnout
Doing the job of an operational manager without receiving the title, authority, or salary that goes with it inevitably breeds deep resentment. You watch less-engaged colleagues advance while you work overtime to clean up their administrative messes. This imbalance is a primary catalyst for quiet burnout.

How to Identify If You Are Stuck in the Glue Work Loop

Many professionals do not realize they are trapped in glue work until years of career stagnation have already passed. To assess whether invisible labor is quietly draining your career trajectory, ask yourself the following diagnostic questions:

  • Are you the default note-taker or meeting host? If no one explicitly assigns administrative roles, do eyes automatically turn toward you?
  • Do colleagues consult you instead of official documentation? Are you constantly interrupting your deep work focus to act as a human help desk for your team?
  • Does your daily routine conflict with your performance metrics? When you look at your job description or yearly goals, are you spending more than 20% of your time on activities that are not listed on your review criteria?
  • Have you received verbal praise that lacks career progression? Do supervisors frequently tell you "we couldn't survive without you," while simultaneously stalling your promotion requests or salary raises?
  • Are you working extra hours to finish your real job? Do you complete administrative team upkeep during standard work hours, forcing you to complete your actual deliverables during evenings or weekends?

If you answered "yes" to three or more of these statements, you are likely carrying an unsustainable volume of invisible glue work.

How to Transition From Invisible Glue to Recognized High Impact

Escaping the Glue Work Dilemma does not mean you have to transform into an unhelpful, selfish colleague. Rather, it requires shifting from unconscious, invisible labor to strategic, recognized contribution. Here is how to reclaim your time, set firm boundaries, and ensure your career trajectory reflects your actual value.

1. Conduct a Two-Week Time Audit
Before you can change your workload, you need data. For the next two weeks, track every task you complete and categorize it into three buckets: Promotable/Core Work (direct goals), Shared Operations (necessary team tasks), and Invisible Glue (unprompted maintenance, quick favors, admin tasks). If your glue work exceeds 15% of your total work week, you need to rebalance.

2. Institute the "Rotation Rule"
Stop stepping into silence. In meetings, when a volunteer is requested to take notes, draft a summary, or coordinate follow-ups, refrain from speaking up immediately. If silence lingers, suggest a system: "To ensure everyone gets equal time to participate, let us establish a rotating schedule for notes and meeting admin every week." This institutionalizes fairness without making you look uncooperative.

3. Convert Glue Work into Formal Accomplishments or Drop It
If glue work is critical to the company, it deserves an explicit title, dedicated resources, and formal recognition. Bring your time audit to your manager during your next 1-on-1. Frame the conversation around trade-offs: "I currently dedicate approximately 10 hours a week to onboarding and internal documentation. While this helps new hires, it reduces the time I can dedicate to [High-Impact Project A]. Should we formalize this operational role as an official priority with metric targets, delegate it, or scale it back so I can focus on [High-Impact Project A]?"

4. Reframe Operational Work using Strategic Metrics
If you must perform glue work, never list it on your resume or performance review as passive help. Translate invisible labor into business impact language. Instead of writing "Helped train new team members," write "Engineered a standardized onboarding workflow that reduced new hire ramp-up time by 25%." Instead of writing "Organized team syncs," write "Restructured cross-departmental communications, reducing meeting overhead by 4 hours per week."

5. Master the Strategic Boundary Script
When asked to take on additional low-impact tasks, do not give an immediate "yes" out of habit. Use prioritization language to protect your bandwidth without coming across as dismissive. Try saying: "I would love to help with this project, but my main focus this quarter is delivering [Primary Goal]. If I take this on, it will delay [Primary Goal] by two weeks. How would you like me to prioritize these competing demands?" This shifts the responsibility back to leadership to decide if the glue task is truly worth sacrificing core output.

Decentering Workplace Approval to Reclaim Your Trajectory

At its core, the tendency to absorb excessive glue work is often driven by psychological factors: approval-seeking, fear of conflict, or an instinct to fix visible problems before anyone else suffers. We want to be liked, valued, and viewed as indispensable.

However, true career security does not come from being liked for taking on extra chores; it comes from building specialized skills, producing clear results, and advocating for fair compensation. Being "nice" at work will never substitute for clear boundaries, strategic positioning, and self-advocacy.

When you step back from glue work, a fascinating thing happens: other people step up. Processes adapt. The organization learns to distribute labor more equitably. And most importantly, you free up the mental bandwidth required to do deep, meaningful work that advances your career on your own terms.
